US Policy Path Lab

A rule-based path for US cash rates

Working paper for the capital markets line · US first, G4 to follow · October 2026 · Interactive: every figure and number marked like this updates with the inputs in the lab
Abstract

We need a path for the US policy rate that turns house forecasts of growth and inflation into an expected cash return over five and ten years, and that can be re-run under alternative scenarios. We set out what the monetary-policy literature supports on each design choice and combine the answers into one quarterly rule: an inertial, error-correcting policy rule that responds to core inflation and to growth relative to potential, converges to a fixed house anchor for the long-run policy rate, and is implemented in 25bp steps at FOMC meetings. Every parameter is calibrated to published estimates of the Fed's reaction function rather than estimated afresh. On the current inputs the path averages –% over five years and –% over ten. The speed parameters change the timing of the path but move these averages by only a few basis points; the anchor moves them almost one for one, and the choice between core and headline inflation is the largest near-term judgement.

1. Purpose

The cash leg of the capital markets line needs the expected average return on cash over five and ten years. Two shortcuts are available and both are weak. Market forwards embed time-varying money-market premia that are hard to separate from expectations (Ferrero and Nobili 2009), and survey and market expectations of the short rate make large, cyclical errors (Cieslak 2018). Copying either also leaves no room for a house view. A path derived from house forecasts avoids both problems, provided the mapping from forecasts to rates is explicit, grounded in evidence on how the Fed actually behaves, and robust to the quantities nobody observes well.

Four requirements follow. The path must start from where the Fed is today; it must respond to growth and inflation in the way the Fed has historically responded; it must converge to a stated long-run rate; and it must be reproducible under any alternative path for growth, headline and core inflation. Section 2 takes each design choice in turn and sets out what the literature supports. Section 3 states the rule and its calibration. Sections 4 to 6 cover inputs, results and sensitivity.

2. What the literature supports

2.1 A policy rule as a description of the Fed

Taylor (1993) proposed a simple rule, r = p + 0.5y + 0.5(p − 2) + 2, in which the funds rate responds by 1.5 to inflation (1 + 0.5) and by 0.5 to the output gap, with a 2% equilibrium real rate and a 2% inflation target. He showed that it fitted Fed policy "remarkably well" over 1987–92. A large empirical literature has since estimated the Fed's reaction function directly. Three findings are robust and shape the design here. First, since 1979 the Fed has raised nominal rates more than one for one with expected inflation, satisfying the Taylor principle, whereas before 1979 it did not: Clarida, Galí and Gertler (2000) estimate an inflation response of 2.15 for 1979Q3–1996Q4 against 0.83 for 1960Q1–1979Q2. Single-equation estimates on samples starting in 1987 put the long-run inflation response at about 1.3–1.6 (English, Nelson and Sack 2003; Rudebusch 2006; Carvalho, Nechio and Tristão 2021), while forward-looking and model-based estimates are nearer 2 (Clarida, Galí and Gertler 2000; Smets and Wouters 2007; Coibion and Gorodnichenko 2012). Second, the reaction function is not fixed. González-Astudillo and Tanvir (2023), estimating it from SEP medians released between June 2012 and March 2023, find the inflation response rising from about 1.6 to 3.6, and persistence rising from late 2016, peaking in 2020 and remaining higher than at the start. Third, what matters for market pricing is the rule investors perceive, which also varies. Bauer, Pflueger and Sunderam (2024) find that forecasters see the Fed as highly data-dependent in tightenings, with a low perceived response to the output gap before and during easings, and that rate cuts surprise forecasters more often than rate hikes.

We therefore treat the rule as a calibrated description of average behaviour since the late 1980s, with ranges wide enough to span the estimates, and allow the response coefficients to change by scenario where the regime itself is part of the scenario, as Pflueger (2025) shows it must be for stagflationary episodes.

2.2 Unobservables: level gaps versus differences

A conventional Taylor rule needs two quantities nobody observes: the neutral real rate and the output gap. Reaction functions estimated on revised data give a misleading picture of policy as it was made (Orphanides 2001), and revisions to real-time output-gap estimates are as large as the gaps themselves, mostly because end-of-sample trend estimates are unreliable (Orphanides and van Norden 2002), and filter-based neutral-rate estimates are imprecise within any single model and move with the potential-output estimates behind them (Laubach and Williams 2003; Beyer and Wieland 2019). Hamilton et al. (2016) find the link between the equilibrium rate and trend growth far weaker than commonly assumed. Orphanides and Williams (2002) show that when natural rates are mismeasured, rules written in differences, which respond to inflation and to the change in the output gap, are robust where level rules perform badly. Walsh (2003) shows that a central bank acting under discretion but stabilising inflation and the change in the output gap (a "speed limit") can reproduce the optimal commitment outcome and does better than inflation targeting. The Board's reference rules include a first-difference rule that responds to the inflation gap and to four-quarter growth of real GDP, on the stated grounds that its prescriptions "do not depend on estimates of unobserved variables" (Board of Governors n.d.).

The change in the output gap is simply growth minus potential growth, which is observable up to the potential-growth assumption. Our rule uses that term in place of the output-gap level, and it needs no estimate of today's neutral rate. The level information a pure difference rule lacks is supplied separately, by the anchor (2.5).

2.3 Inertia: partial adjustment and momentum

Estimated rules almost always include the lagged policy rate with a large coefficient. Woodford (1999, 2003) shows why a central bank might choose this: under optimal policy, responding to lagged variables shapes private expectations of future policy, and a given degree of interest-rate variability then has a larger effect on long rates and demand. Empirically, quarterly smoothing estimates since the late 1980s lie between about 0.72 and 0.83 (Rudebusch 2002; English, Nelson and Sack 2003; Coibion and Gorodnichenko 2012; Carvalho, Nechio and Tristão 2021), and the Board's inertial reference rule uses 0.85. Two-lag and error-correction specifications also find momentum in changes. Judd and Rudebusch (1998) estimate that the Greenspan Fed closed 28% of the gap to its target each quarter and carried over 42% of the previous quarter's change. Coibion and Gorodnichenko's (2012) two-lag estimate on Greenbook forecasts (lag coefficients 1.22 and −0.40) maps in our notation to an adjustment speed of 0.18 and momentum of 0.40.

Whether this is genuine smoothing is disputed. Rudebusch (2002, 2006) argues that inertia of that size would imply far more predictable variation in interest rates beyond three months than the term structure shows, and that a rule with serially correlated shocks fits the evidence better. English, Nelson and Sack (2003) find both smoothing and serially correlated shocks; allowing for the shocks lowers estimated smoothing from 0.72 to 0.60, an effect they describe as small. Lansing (2002) shows that when true smoothing is zero, real-time mismeasurement of trend output produces spurious estimates of about 0.3–0.4. Coibion and Gorodnichenko (2012), using the Fed's own Greenbook forecasts, conclude that the evidence strongly favours genuine smoothing. For our purpose the dispute matters less than it seems: Section 6 shows the adjustment speed changes the timing of the path but moves five- and ten-year averages by a few basis points. We take the error-correction estimates, which match our functional form exactly, and set the range wide enough to include the faster adjustment the sceptics imply.

2.4 Which inflation

Theory says a central bank should target inflation in the sticky-price sector rather than a broad measure (Aoki 2001), and give less weight to sectors whose prices are more flexible, energy among them (Mankiw and Reis 2003; in their US calibration the optimal index also puts substantial weight on nominal wages). Empirical reaction functions are accordingly estimated on core or deflator measures, not headline (Rudebusch 2006; Carvalho, Nechio and Tristão 2021). Our default responds to core CPI (θ = 1). The share of headline the Fed responds to, (1 − θ), is a scenario choice: a Fed that judges an energy shock to be feeding into expectations behaves more like θ = 0, and Pflueger (2025) shows that this choice, together with how reactive policy is, decides whether bonds hedge equities in a stagflationary episode. In the current episode, with headline at 3.7% and core at 2.6% in September 2026, it is the largest single near-term judgement in the framework (Section 6).

2.5 Anchoring and mean reversion

Short rates are highly persistent, and their long-horizon expectations move with perceived long-run inflation and the long-run real rate. Kozicki and Tinsley (2001) show that term-structure models better explain yield fluctuations when the endpoint of the short rate shifts with perceptions of the policy inflation target, and Bauer and Rudebusch (2020) show that accounting for time variation in the long-run trends of inflation and the real rate is crucial for explaining and forecasting Treasury yields. A forecasting rule therefore needs an explicit endpoint, and the choice is between estimating it and setting it. Given the imprecision of model-based estimates (2.2), we set it: the long-run fed funds rate is a house input, here 3.75%, with long-run CPI inflation of 2.6%. Its determinants are a separate module.

In the rule the anchor enters through the error-correction term, so the speed of mean reversion is the adjustment speed λ together with the momentum ρ. With gaps closed, a deviation from the anchor follows xt = (1 + ρ − λ)xt−1 − ρxt−2. At the central calibration this halves in about 3 quarters with a small overshoot (Figure 4). Beyond the forecast horizon, inflation and growth gaps are assumed to close with a one-year half-life, so the path reaches the anchor within two to three years of the last forecast year. Two consistency conditions follow. The anchor's inflation component must equal long-run inflation in the forecasts, on the same price index, or the rate never reaches the anchor; and the long-run inflation response 1 + φπ must exceed one.

2.6 Implementation: steps, meetings, the lower bound and the starting stance

The funds target moves in discrete 25bp steps at scheduled meetings, and the timing of changes has its own dynamics (Hamilton and Jordà 2002). We run the rule on a continuous intended rate and implement it on the grid of range midpoints, moving only when the intended rate is more than half a step away and at most 25bp per meeting, over the whole horizon. The anchor must therefore be a reachable midpoint. A point anchor of 3.75% is the boundary between the 3.50–3.75% and 3.75–4.00% ranges, so a stepped path settles in whichever range it approaches from and stays there. That makes long-run averages depend on the direction of approach and can reverse the ranking of scenarios. The house anchor is therefore expressed as the 3.75–4.00% range, with a midpoint of 3.875%. The effective lower bound is imposed on the implemented rate while the intended rate is allowed to go below it, so the rule remembers how far policy would have gone and lifts off later. This is a simple version of the make-up policies studied by Reifschneider and Williams (2000), whose rule keeps rates lower after a lower-bound episode in proportion to the cumulative shortfall of past policy.

The rule closes the gap between today's rate and our target. If the Fed's own target differs, for example because it responds to an output-gap level, an optional stance term s0 lets the near-term target follow the Fed's view and decay towards ours. It can be estimated by inverting the SEP median path through the rule. It is set to zero here because the Fed's September projections and the baseline path are close.

2.7 Forecasts, markets and benchmarks

A rule driven by house forecasts will not beat market pricing at short horizons. Over 1987–97, Evans (1998) found that a Taylor rule fitted to revised data predicted the funds rate about as well as fed funds futures, but the best real-time rule produced errors about 50% larger. That is the price of keeping money-market premia and consensus out of the path, and it matters little for five- and ten-year averages, which are dominated by the anchor (Section 6). Market forwards, the SEP and survey forecasts are therefore shown as benchmarks for explaining divergence and for back-testing, not used as inputs. AQR (2024) take the opposite approach, setting expected cash returns to a simple average of the three-month yield, the ten-year yield and the survey forecast of the average short rate over the next decade, less forecast inflation; that is a useful external check on the result.

Equation (1) is the error-correction form of an inertial Taylor rule (Judd and Rudebusch 1998): each quarter the rate closes a share λ of the gap to a target equal to the anchor plus responses to inflation and to growth relative to potential, and carries forward a share ρ of last quarter's change. A one-lag smoothing coefficient s maps to λ = 1 − s; a two-lag rule with coefficients s1, s2 maps to λ = 1 − s1 − s2 and ρ = −s2. In steady state the rate equals ī + (1 + φπ)(π − π*) + φg(g − g*), so persistent gaps move the long-run rate, and the anchor is reached only when inflation and growth settle at their long-run values.

4. Inputs and conventions

Annual averages. House and consensus forecasts are calendar-year averages of year-on-year rates. A quarterly path is needed, so the framework finds the smoothest quarterly path whose calendar-year averages equal the forecasts, including quarters already observed. This matters: with headline CPI at 2.7%, 3.8% and 3.5% in the first three quarters of 2026, a 3.6% average for 2026 would require 4.4% in the fourth quarter. Reading annual averages as year-end rates shifts the path by about half a year.

Base effects and the October 2026 house round. The March–May 2026 energy spike (monthly increases of 0.9%, 0.6% and 0.5%) drops out of the twelve-month comparison in March–May 2027, and the June 2026 fall (−0.4%) in June 2027. Year-on-year inflation in 2027 therefore dips and rebounds regardless of underlying momentum. The CPI builder makes this explicit by constructing headline and core month by month from seasonally adjusted changes, with core momentum, energy and food as the assumptions. The house round has headline CPI at 3.6% in 2026, 3.2% in 2027 and 2.6% thereafter. With oil flat at its September level, the builder gives 3.5% and 2.95%. Brent rose about 11% in October, and an energy-price rise of about 5% that month, flat thereafter, with core momentum unchanged (2.8% easing to 2.6%), reproduces the house figures: headline averages –% in 2026 and –% in 2027, about 4.3% in the fourth quarter. The revision is therefore an energy revision. Core, and with it the baseline path, which responds to core, is unchanged; the headline-reactive scenario is where the higher profile shows.

Potential growth. The growth term uses the house potential growth rate, 2.1%. Consistency matters as much as the forecast: if growth is forecast at 2.1% indefinitely while potential is taken from CBO (1.8% from 2031), the rule reads permanent overheating and settles about 20bp above the anchor.

6. Sensitivity

Table 2 moves each parameter to the ends of its evidence range for the selected scenario. Three results hold across scenarios. The adjustment speed and momentum change the timing and the peak, but move ten-year averages by a few basis points, because they govern how fast the rate travels, not where it ends up. The anchor moves averages almost one for one, and with it the long-run inflation and potential-growth assumptions that define it. The inflation measure (θ) is the largest near-term judgement when headline and core diverge, as they do now.

7. Limitations and next steps

The rule is calibrated, not estimated, and should be checked against history: run it on real-time SPF forecasts and realised fed funds since 1990 and record where it misses (the 2008–15 lower-bound period and the 2021–22 delay are the obvious candidates). The growth response has the thinnest evidence and the widest range. The rule has no term for financial conditions, the balance sheet or forward guidance; persistent deviations of that kind belong in scenarios. The anchor is an input, and its determinants are a separate piece of work. Extending to the euro area, UK and Japan needs country calibrations; Japan in particular has little post-lower-bound history and should carry a wider band.
