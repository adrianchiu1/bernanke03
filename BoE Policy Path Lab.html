A rule-based path for UK cash rates

Working paper for the capital markets line · United Kingdom, companion to the US and euro-area papers · October 2026 · Interactive: every figure and number marked like this updates with the inputs in the lab
Abstract

We extend the rule-based cash-rate framework to Bank Rate. The structure is unchanged: an error-correcting policy rule that responds to projected inflation and to growth relative to potential, converges to a fixed house anchor and moves in 25bp steps at MPC meetings. The calibration comes from about a dozen UK studies and the Bank of England's own models. That evidence is as deep as the euro-area evidence on smoothing and the inflation response, but thin on the growth response and on momentum, and no published estimate covers the period after 2010. On the house forecasts as given (3.0% anchor, long-run CPI 2.3%), Bank Rate averages –% over five years and –% over ten. The house view implies gentle cuts from today's 3.75%, while money markets price about four hikes. Three things account for the difference: an inflation path below the Bank's own near-term guidance, a long-run inflation assumption above the 2% target, and whether the MPC looks through the energy shock. Each is a scenario in the lab.

1. Purpose and what differs

The cash leg of the capital markets line needs the expected average return on sterling cash over five and ten years, derived from house forecasts rather than copied from the SONIA curve. The case for that, and for the error-correction structure, is in the US paper. This paper sets out what the UK evidence supports and where it is thin.

Three things differ from the US and euro area. The evidence base is shorter. Inflation targeting began in October 1992 and operational independence in 1997, and Bank Rate sat at or near its floor from 2009 to 2021, so almost all estimates use 1992–2007 data. The target variable changed in December 2003, from RPIX at 2.5% to CPI at 2%. And the starting point is contested inside the MPC. Bank Rate has been 3.75% since December 2025. In September 2026 the MPC held it by 6–3, with three members voting to raise it (Bank of England 2026). Headline CPI was 3.5% in September, core 2.8% and services 3.7%, and money markets price about four quarter-point rises by mid-2027.

2. What the literature supports

2.1 The MPC's reaction function

Before inflation targeting, UK policy did not satisfy the Taylor principle. Clarida, Galí and Gertler (1998) estimate an inflation response of 0.48 for 1979–90, with UK rates heavily influenced by German rates. Since 1992 the estimates cluster around 1.5. Nelson (2000) finds 1.27 for 1992–97, on only 18 quarters. Martin and Milas (2004) find 1.45–1.65 for 1992–2003, and the linear version of Boinet and Martin's (2008) model gives 1.71. Chadha, Sarno and Valente (2004), on a 1979–2000 sample that straddles the regime change, find 1.03. Adam, Cobham and Girardin (2005) find 1.89 for 1997–2002. Cobham and Kang (2013) find 1.86–2.13 for 1997–2007, though their instruments are weak. The Bank's models sit at the lower end: 1.4 in COMPASS (Burgess et al. 2013) and 1.28 in DiCecio and Nelson (2007). The illustrative rules the Bank has published since November 2025 use 1.5, calibrated to align with the Federal Reserve's 2019 Tealbook rules rather than estimated on UK data (Bank of England 2025a, 2025b). We take 1.5, with a range of 1.2–2.1.

Two qualifications matter for the current episode. First, several studies find the response is nonlinear. Martin and Milas (2004) and Boinet and Martin (2008) estimate a stronger response when inflation leaves a zone around the target (1.3–2.8% in Martin and Milas). Taylor and Davradakis (2006) find the rule bites only when expected inflation is well above target. A linear rule will therefore understate the MPC's response to a large overshoot. Second, the only study that extends beyond 2007, Martin and Milas (2013), finds the inflation response falls sharply and becomes insignificant after 2007, as Bank Rate reached its floor and asset purchases took over.

2.2 Growth rather than the output gap

UK estimates mostly use an output-gap level, with long-run responses of 0.35–0.54 in most studies (Nelson 2000; Chadha, Sarno and Valente 2004; Martin and Milas 2004; DiCecio and Nelson 2007). Evidence on growth is thin. Goodhart (2005), using the Bank's Inflation Report forecasts for 1997–2003, finds forecast growth insignificant and wrongly signed. Cobham and Kang (2013), using the same forecasts in first differences, find a quarterly response of 0.17–0.19 to forecast growth relative to trend. Cloyne and Hürtgen (2016), modelling each decision against the forecasts available at the time, find a significant response to growth forecast one quarter ahead. Besley, Meads and Surico (2008) find the output-gap forecast insignificant in individual MPC votes. The Bank's illustrative first-difference rule puts 0.1 on GDP growth relative to its steady state, following the Fed's rule.

The UK evidence alone would not justify replacing the gap with growth. The case rests on the general argument of the US paper: output-gap estimates are unreliable in real time, and difference rules are robust to that error (Orphanides and Williams 2002; Walsh 2003). The UK estimates are consistent with it rather than proof of it. We use the US value of 0.75, which gives a quarterly impact of 0.15, between the Bank's 0.1 and Cobham and Kang's 0.17–0.19. The range of 0.4–1.1 reflects how few estimates there are.

2.3 Inertia and momentum

Quarterly smoothing estimates imply an adjustment speed of 0.1–0.4 in our notation: 0.11 in Chadha, Sarno and Valente (2004), 0.13 in DiCecio and Nelson (2007), 0.15 in the Bank's illustrative inertial rule, 0.20 in COMPASS, 0.23–0.24 in Martin and Milas (2004), and 0.37 in Boinet and Martin's (2008) two-lag estimate. The COMPASS posterior for smoothing equals its prior mean, so it adds little information. Nelson's (2000) 0.71 comes from 18 quarters and is not used. We take 0.20 with a range of 0.10–0.40.

Momentum is where the UK evidence conflicts. The one two-lag estimate implies momentum of 0.50 (Boinet and Martin 2008). The forecast-based studies find the lagged change insignificant: Goodhart's (2005) lagged changes are 0.02 and −0.17, and Cobham and Kang (2013) find the same. Cloyne and Hürtgen (2016) find no response to the starting level of Bank Rate once forecasts are included. Cobham (2003) argues that much of the apparent smoothing reflects serially correlated shocks rather than deliberate gradualism. We set momentum at 0.20, between the two classes of evidence, with a range of 0–0.50. Section 6 shows it changes timing, not averages.

2.4 Which inflation, and at what horizon

All the UK estimates use headline measures: RPI, RPIX or CPI, mostly as forecasts. The horizon that fits best is three to four quarters ahead in GMM estimates (Cobham and Kang 2013). Studies that use the Bank's own forecasts find seven to eight quarters. Those coefficients are inflated, because the Bank's forecasts are conditioned on market rates and so already embed the expected policy response (Goodhart 2005). The Bank's illustrative rules use three and five quarters. The rule therefore reads projected headline CPI four quarters ahead and growth one quarter ahead.

A forecast lead looks through temporary energy effects only if the forecast does. The UK energy profile is unusually lumpy, because the Ofgem price cap resets each quarter. The cap rose 13% in July 2026 and 4% in October, and forecasts for January 2027 range from about 16% to over 20% (Ofgem 2026a, 2026b; Cornwall Insight 2026). The Bank expects CPI to be about 3¾% in the fourth quarter and slightly above 4% in the first quarter of 2027. The hawkish dissents argue that this is feeding into wages and expectations (Bank of England 2026). An MPC that acted on current rather than projected inflation would raise rates sharply; Section 6 shows how sharply.

2.5 Anchoring

The long-run Bank Rate is a house input, here 3.00%. That is the middle of the 2–4% range for the nominal neutral rate that Ramsden (2025) describes, and the same as the COMPASS staff assumption cited by Taylor (2026), who also gives 3% as his own estimate. The house long-run CPI inflation of 2.3% creates a consistency question the US and euro-area inputs did not. The MPC's target is 2.0% CPI, with no index wedge. A long-run inflation forecast of 2.3% therefore means either that the house expects the MPC to tolerate a permanent 0.3pp overshoot, or that the 3.0% anchor embeds a real neutral rate of 0.7% rather than 1.0%. The baseline uses 2.3% as the rule's inflation objective, so the path settles at the anchor. If the MPC instead aims at 2.0% while inflation settles at 2.3%, the rule reads a permanent gap and settles about 45bp higher (the second scenario). With all gaps closed, a deviation from the anchor halves in about 3 quarters (Figure 4).

2.6 Implementation

The MPC meets eight times a year, and two meetings remain in 2026: 5 November and 17 December. Bank Rate moves in multiples of 25bp, and the rule moves at most 25bp per meeting. That cap is a simplification. In March 2020 the MPC cut by 50bp and then 15bp, to the 0.1% floor, and in November 2022 it raised Bank Rate by 75bp. The floor is set at 0.1%, the lowest level to date, with the grid on multiples of 0.25. The 3.0% anchor is a grid point, so there is no parking artefact. Steps apply over the whole horizon, and the stepped path converges to the anchor exactly.

2.7 Filling the post-2008 gap

The UK has one advantage the euro area does not. The Bank has published its inflation and growth projections each quarter since the 1990s, with the market path they are conditioned on. That allows an in-house, forecast-based estimate of the reaction function over 1997–2026, adjusting for the market-rate conditioning as Goodhart (2005) and Cobham and Kang (2013) do. It is the first item in Section 7. The Bernanke (2024) review did not recommend simple rules. It recommended alternative scenarios, including alternative policy paths. Since November 2025 the Bank has published rule-based paths as cross-checks, which gives a second benchmark against which to check this calibration.

4. Inputs and conventions

CPI. CPI averaged 3.0% over January–September 2026 (Office for National Statistics 2026a), so the house 3.1% for 2026 implies a fourth-quarter average of 3.4%. The Bank expects about 3¾% (Bank of England 2026), which would make 2026 about 3.2%. For 2027, the Bank's guidance puts the first quarter slightly above 4%, before the January price-cap reset is known. A 2.7% average for 2027 then needs the remaining three quarters to average about 2.2%. That is possible only if wholesale gas and oil fall back far enough for the July 2026 cap rise and this year's fuel-price increases to drop out of the comparison without being replaced. The third scenario follows the Bank's near-term guidance and then eases to the house 2028 figure; 2027 then averages about 3.6%. There is no monthly CPI builder for the UK yet. The ONS does not publish an aggregate energy index or weight (energy is about 5.8% by subtraction), and household energy moves in quarterly price-cap steps, which need their own module.

GDP. GDP was 0.8% and 1.4% higher than a year earlier in the first and second quarters. Monthly output rose 0.4% in July and 0.1% in August, so the third quarter was probably about 1.6% up on a year earlier (Office for National Statistics 2026b, 2026c). A 1.2% average for 2026 then needs output to fall by about 0.6% in the fourth quarter; with a flat fourth quarter, 2026 is about 1.4%. The gap is smaller than in the euro-area inputs and matters little for the path, but it should be reconciled. The first estimate for the third quarter is due on 12 November, and the 2026 Blue Book revisions were scheduled for 30 October.

Potential growth. The house potential rate of 1.2% is consistent with the house long-run growth forecast, so the growth term closes. The Bank's July 2026 projection has potential supply growth of about 0.3–0.4% a quarter, consistent with that.

5. Results

–

All scenarios run with the current parameters and anchor. Year-end values are Bank Rate where the path is stepped.

On the house forecasts, the rule cuts. Bank Rate is 75bp above the 3.0% anchor. House inflation four quarters ahead is close to the house long-run 2.3%, so nothing offsets the pull towards the anchor, and the path steps down to 3.25% during 2027 and 3.0% by 2029. Three changes move it towards the market path. An MPC aiming at 2.0% while inflation settles at 2.3% holds Bank Rate near 3.5% throughout. Inflation following the Bank's near-term guidance keeps it at 3.75% through 2026. And an MPC that reacts to current rather than projected inflation, in that same scenario, raises Bank Rate to 4.75% by mid-2027, close to market pricing. The market path is therefore consistent with an MPC that does not look through the energy shock. The house path assumes it does, with a lower inflation profile. The s0 slider shows the alternative way to reconcile the two: an MPC whose own near-term target sits above ours.

6. Sensitivity

Table 2 moves each parameter to the ends of its evidence range for the selected scenario. The anchor moves averages almost one for one. The long-run inflation objective π* is the next largest lever: 2.0% rather than 2.3% adds about 40bp to the ten-year average. The forecast horizon decides the near term. Reading current rather than projected inflation turns the house path from cuts into a hold with one rise, to 4.0%. In the Bank's near-term scenario it produces four hikes.

7. Limitations and next steps

The calibration rests on 1992–2007 estimates. The first step is a forecast-based estimate on the Bank's published projections over 1997–2026, adjusting for the market-rate conditioning, which would extend the evidence to the lower-bound period, the 2021–23 tightening and the easing that followed. The growth response and momentum rest on very few studies and carry wide ranges. The rule is linear, while the UK evidence points to a stronger response outside a target zone. The monthly CPI builder needs a price-cap module before it can be used for the UK. Quantitative tightening, now including active gilt sales, and the term premium are outside the rule.
