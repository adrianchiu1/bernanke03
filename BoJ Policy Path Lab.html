A rule-based path for yen cash rates

Working paper for the capital markets line · Japan, companion to the US, euro-area and UK papers · November 2026 · Provisional: no house forecasts for Japan yet; inputs are Bank of Japan medians · Interactive: every figure and number marked like this updates with the inputs in the lab
Abstract

We extend the rule-based cash-rate framework to the Bank of Japan's policy rate, the uncollateralised overnight call rate. The structure is unchanged: an error-correcting rule that responds to projected inflation and to growth relative to potential, converges to a fixed anchor and is implemented in 25bp steps at the eight Monetary Policy Meetings a year. The calibration is the hardest of the four markets. The policy rate was at or below zero from 1999 to 2024 with brief exceptions, so most of the reaction-function literature either predates the modern framework or has to censor the lower bound, and the two best recent sources describe perceptions of the rule rather than the rule itself. We take a forward-looking specification with no momentum, as for the ECB, an inflation response at the Taylor-principle value, an anchor at the midpoint of the Bank's own neutral range, and a rule that reads inflation on the Bank's underlying measure, CPI excluding fresh food and energy adjusted for institutional factors. On the October 2026 Outlook Report medians the rule raises the call rate to 2.00% during 2027 and settles at the 1.75% anchor by 2029, averaging –% over five years and –% over ten. The anchor is the dominant uncertainty: the Bank's own range for the nominal neutral rate spans 1.1–2.5%.

1. Purpose and what differs

The cash leg of the capital markets line needs the expected average return on yen cash over five and ten years, derived from forecasts rather than copied from the OIS curve. The case for that, and for the error-correction structure, is set out in the US paper. This paper sets out what the Japanese evidence supports, which is less than for any other market, and where judgement has to fill the gap.

Four things differ. First, the history. The uncollateralised call rate was at zero from 1999 to 2006, at 0.5% or below from 2008, negative from 2016 to 2024, and only returned to positive territory in March 2024. A conventional reaction function cannot be estimated on most of this sample, and Kuttner and Posen (2004) and Ito and Mishkin (2004) argue that estimated Taylor rules say little about Japanese policy at all. Second, the target. The 2% price stability target is defined on all-items CPI, but the Bank forecasts, communicates and assesses progress on CPI excluding fresh food, and since 2015 has emphasised CPI excluding fresh food and energy as the measure of underlying inflation (Bank of Japan 2026c). In March 2026 it began publishing both series adjusted for institutional factors such as energy subsidies and free education. Third, the starting point. The policy rate was raised to 1.25% in September 2026 and held on 30 October by 8–1, the dissent for 1.50%; the Bank says it "will continue to raise the policy interest rate and adjust the degree of monetary accommodation" and that the risk to underlying inflation is now to the upside (Bank of Japan 2026a, 2026b). Fourth, the inputs. There are no house forecasts for Japan yet, so the baseline uses the Policy Board medians from the October 2026 Outlook Report, mapped approximately from fiscal to calendar years. Everything in this paper should be read as provisional until house forecasts replace them.

2. What the literature supports

2.1 The Bank of Japan's reaction function

Four sources carry most of the weight. Jinushi, Kuroki and Miyao (2000) estimate a "good" policy rule on 1975–85, when the Bank was disinflating successfully, with a lagged dependent variable. Their basic GMM specification puts 0.39 on the lagged rate, an adjustment speed of about 0.6 a quarter, with long-run responses of 0.45 to the inflation gap and 1.9 to the output gap; the OLS variants put 0.65–0.66 on the lag (adjustment 0.35) with long-run responses of about 0.5 and 2.0. The inflation response fails the Taylor principle, which is a feature of the period rather than a guide to the present. Applying the rule forward, they identify delayed policy action in 1987–88, 1992–95 and 1997–98. Ito and Mishkin (2004) estimate static rules on 1982–94 with a 2% target: backward-looking responses of 1.47 on the GDP deflator and 1.75 on CPI excluding fresh food, with output-gap responses of 0.21 and 0.31; forward-looking responses of 1.23 and 1.71 with the gap insignificant. They conclude that Taylor-rule estimates are a poor instrument for judging Japanese policy, a view shared by Kuttner and Posen (2004).

Hayashi and Koeda (2019) solve the lower-bound problem directly. They estimate a censored Taylor rule with an explicit exit condition on monthly data for 1992–2012 by maximum likelihood. The speed of adjustment is 7.8% a month, about 0.22 a quarter in our notation; the inflation and output coefficients in the desired rate are 1.01 and 0.04. A Tobit estimate that ignores the exit condition gives a lower speed, 3.8% a month, because it reads the Bank's refusal to exit QE while the desired rate was positive as smoothing. The point generalises: any estimate on a sample that includes the zero-rate years will overstate inertia unless the exit rule is modelled.

The most recent evidence is on perceptions rather than behaviour. Haba, Hirano, Ito and Kaihatsu (2026), Bank of Japan staff, estimate the reaction function as perceived by market participants from the QUICK bond survey over 2004–25. The inflation coefficient is near zero while the rate is at the lower bound, rose during the 2006–07 tightening and when negative rates were introduced in 2016, and rose markedly after yield-curve control was loosened in July and October 2023. Participants whose long-run inflation expectations sit below 2% continue to perceive a weak response. The specification has no activity term and no smoothing, so it informs the direction of the calibration, not its parameters. Clarida, Galí and Gertler (1998) also estimate a forward-looking rule for the Bank over 1979–94, with monthly smoothing above 0.9 and an inflation response of about 2; we cite it for completeness and have not re-verified the published table.

These estimates span 0.45 to about 2 for the long-run inflation response and 0.2 to 0.6 for the adjustment speed, on samples that end before the current framework. The Bank's own communication is the better guide to the present. Since underlying inflation reached a level "generally consistent with" 2%, the Bank has framed policy as a sequence of adjustments toward neutral whose "timing and pace" depend on the outlook, and has shifted its stated risk from downside to upside (Bank of Japan 2026b). That is a forward-looking rule with a positive inflation response and no commitment to a particular speed.

2.2 Growth rather than the output gap

Every Japanese estimate uses an output-gap level, and the Bank publishes its own output gap and potential growth estimates, with the caveat that both "should be interpreted with considerable latitude" (Bank of Japan 2026b). The real-time problem that motivates growth rules in the US paper is worse in Japan: potential growth has been revised repeatedly and the Bank currently puts it at "around 0.5–1.0 percent" under one methodology. The forward-looking estimates of Ito and Mishkin find the gap insignificant once expected inflation is included, and Hayashi and Koeda's output coefficient is 0.04. We therefore keep the US design, growth minus potential, with the anchor supplying the level information. There is no direct Japanese evidence on the growth response; we use the US value of 0.75 with a wide range of 0.3–1.1.

2.3 Inertia without momentum

Hayashi and Koeda's 0.22 a quarter is the only adjustment-speed estimate that handles the lower bound, and it is close to the US and UK values. Jinushi et al.'s faster speeds come from a high-inflation decade with large, frequent rate moves. The Bank's current practice, 25bp steps separated by two or more meetings, is consistent with a speed of about 0.2 rather than 0.6. We take 0.20 with a range of 0.10–0.30. There is no Japanese evidence on momentum in rate changes; the Bank's 2024–26 hikes have been deliberately spaced rather than clustered, and the one two-lag estimate elsewhere (Boinet and Martin for the UK) does not transfer. We set ρ = 0, as for the ECB, with a range of 0–0.3.

2.4 Which inflation, and at what horizon

Japan has the most layered set of inflation measures of the four markets. The target is 2% on all-items CPI. The Bank's forecasts and its assessment of progress are on CPI excluding fresh food, which Japanese usage calls "core". Since the 2014–15 oil shock the Bank has published and emphasised CPI excluding fresh food and energy, "core-core", and the October 2026 Outlook Report forecasts both (Bank of Japan 2026b). The Western core, all items less food and energy, is compiled by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications but used less, because food is a large weight in the Japanese basket and the Bank argues that recent food inflation reflects labour and distribution costs rather than supply noise (Bank of Japan 2026c). Since March 2026 each exclusion measure is also published excluding institutional factors: consumption-tax changes, free education and school lunches, the 2021 mobile-phone charge cut, travel subsidies and energy-burden relief. Alongside these the Bank monitors a 10% trimmed mean, a weighted median, the mode of the item-level distribution and a diffusion index, which Hogen, Kawamoto and Nakahama (2015) show track the output gap much more weakly than the exclusion measures, and four model-based estimates of trend inflation.

For the rule, "headline" is CPI excluding fresh food and "core" is CPI excluding fresh food and energy, both institutional-factor-adjusted. The Bank's decision language is entirely about underlying inflation, so the central θ is 1; a rule that reacts to the ex-fresh-food measure (θ = 0) is a scenario. This matters now: the proposed temporary cut in consumption tax on food would lower fiscal-2027 inflation by about 1.3 points on both measures, and a rule run on unadjusted data would call for easing in response to a tax cut. The horizon follows the ECB: the Bank's forecasts are two to three years forward and its statements condition on the "likelihood of realizing the baseline scenario", so the rule reads projected inflation four quarters ahead and growth two quarters ahead. The leads can be changed in the lab.

2.5 Anchoring and mean reversion

The long-run policy rate is an input, here 1.75%, with long-run core inflation of 2.0%, so a long-run real rate of about −¼%. The Bank's latest estimate of the nominal neutral rate is 1.1–2.5%, a real rate of −0.9 to +0.5%, and it stresses that the range is wide and should not be read as a target; one board member has argued it may be pushed higher by overseas rates. We take the midpoint rounded to the grid. The IMF's April 2026 Article IV projects the policy rate reaching about 1.5% by end-2027, and private forecasters' terminal-rate assumptions cluster at 1.5%, so 1.75% sits slightly above consensus. The anchor is the dominant uncertainty in this market; Table 2 shows that moving it by half a point moves the ten-year average by about 43bp. With all gaps closed, a deviation from the anchor decays as xt = (1 + ρ − λ)xt−1 − ρxt−2. With no momentum, convergence has no overshoot, and a gap halves in about 4 quarters (Figure 4).

2.6 Implementation: steps, meetings and the lower bound

The call-rate target moves in multiples of 25bp at eight Monetary Policy Meetings a year. Only 18–19 December remains in 2026, and the 2027 calendar has two meetings in each quarter. The rule runs on a continuous intended rate and implements it on the 25bp grid, moving only when the intended rate is more than half a step away and at most 25bp per meeting. The effective lower bound is set at −0.10%, the level of the 2016–24 negative-rate policy, on the implemented rate; the intended rate may go below it, so the rule remembers how far policy would have gone. This is the simple make-up feature of the US paper. It has not been back-tested on the 1999–2024 episode, and the Hayashi–Koeda result that exit depended on an explicit inflation condition rather than on the desired rate alone suggests the rule would have lifted off too early in 2006 and too early again in 2013. A back-test is the first item in Section 7.

2.7 Fiscal years and institutional factors

The Bank's forecasts are for fiscal years ending in March. The lab's inputs are calendar-year averages, so the medians are mapped approximately: fiscal 2026 to calendar 2026 using the three observed quarters, fiscal 2027 to calendar 2027, and so on. The error is largest in 2026, where calendar-year core CPI excluding fresh food has averaged about 1.9% through September against a fiscal-year median of 2.5% that includes a fourth quarter boosted by energy and the yen. The October Report's forecasts also exclude the consumption-tax cut; the rule should run on institutional-factor-adjusted series so that this does not matter. When house forecasts for Japan arrive, both conversions should be done explicitly.

4. Inputs and conventions

Growth in 2026. Real GDP rose 0.4% in the second quarter (1.4% annualised, second estimate), and the Bank expects fiscal 2026 growth of 0.7%, "at a decelerated rate" because of the rise in energy and raw-material prices since the spring, with growth rising moderately from fiscal 2027 as those effects fade and AI-related demand and wage growth support spending (Bank of Japan 2026b). The calendar-year inputs are 1.0% for 2026, reflecting the stronger first half, then the fiscal medians of 0.7 and 0.8%, decaying to potential of 0.6%.

The 2026 inflation figure. CPI excluding fresh food was 2.1% in September 2026, the first reading at or above 2% in nine months, after 1.7% in August; excluding fresh food and energy it was 2.4%, up from 1.9%. The calendar-year average through September is about 1.9% on the ex-fresh-food measure, so the fiscal-2026 median of 2.5% requires a fourth quarter and first quarter of 2027 well above 2%, which the Bank attributes to crude oil, semiconductor prices and the yen. The calendar-year inputs are 2.0% for 2026 on the ex-fresh-food measure and 2.2% on the ex-fresh-food-and-energy measure, then the fiscal medians.

Potential and consistency. Potential growth of 0.6% is inside the Bank's range and long-run inflation equals the 2% target on the underlying measure, so the path settles at the anchor. The anchor of 1.75% implies a long-run real rate of about −¼%, inside the Bank's −0.9 to +0.5% range. The quarterly CPI history in the lab is approximate (monthly data averaged by hand) and should be replaced with the Bank's institutional-factor-adjusted series.

5. Results

–

All scenarios run with the current parameters and anchor. Year-end values are the call-rate target where the path is stepped.

Three results stand out across the scenarios. First, on the Bank's own medians the rule continues the normalisation: a hike in December 2026 and two more in the first half of 2027 take the call rate to 2.00%, above the 1.75% anchor, because projected underlying inflation sits at 2.7% in fiscal 2027; the rate returns to the anchor by 2029 as inflation falls back to 2%. Second, the choice of measure matters in the other direction from the US. A rule that reacts to CPI excluding fresh food, which the Bank projects falling to 2.0% in fiscal 2028, stops at 1.75% rather than 2.00% and lowers the five-year average by about 15bp. Third, the consumption-tax scenario shows why the institutional-factor-adjusted series matter: run on unadjusted data the rule skips the December hike and lowers the five-year average by about 12bp in response to a tax cut, which no reading of the Bank's communication supports.

6. Sensitivity

Table 2 moves each parameter to the ends of its evidence range for the selected scenario. The anchor dominates, as in the other markets, and here its uncertainty is widest. The adjustment speed matters more than elsewhere because the starting rate is further from the anchor in proportional terms: moving λ to the bottom of its range lowers the five-year average by about 15bp. The inflation response and the measure matter for the two-year average because projected underlying inflation is above target through 2027. Momentum and the growth response barely matter for any average.

7. Limitations and next steps

Everything here is provisional. The parameters are set from a thin and dated literature and from reading the Bank's communication; none is estimated on the current framework. The inputs are the Bank's medians rather than house forecasts, converted approximately from fiscal to calendar years, and the quarterly CPI history is hand-averaged. Four things should happen before this path is used for the capital markets line. First, house forecasts for Japanese GDP and for both CPI measures, with the fiscal-to-calendar conversion done explicitly. Second, the institutional-factor-adjusted CPI series from the Bank as the inflation inputs, so that the consumption-tax cut and energy subsidies do not enter the rule. Third, a back-test of the rule on the 2006–08 and 2024–26 exits, the only two periods with positive rates in a quarter-century, to discipline the adjustment speed and the lower-bound memory. Fourth, a view on the anchor that is the house's own rather than the midpoint of the Bank's range, since half a point on it is worth 43bp on the ten-year average. The exchange rate, which the Bank names as a risk in every statement and which several Japanese studies include in the rule, is outside the framework; a persistent yen effect belongs in a scenario.
