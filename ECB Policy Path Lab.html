ECB Policy Path Lab

A rule-based path for euro-area cash rates

Working paper for the capital markets line · Euro area, companion to the US paper · October 2026 · Interactive: every figure and number marked like this updates with the inputs in the lab
Abstract

We extend the rule-based cash-rate framework built for the US to the ECB's deposit facility rate. The structure is unchanged: an error-correcting policy rule that responds to inflation and to growth relative to potential, converges to a fixed house anchor and is implemented in 25bp steps at Governing Council meetings. The calibration is not carried over. Euro-area reaction-function estimates differ from US ones in three ways that matter here. The ECB responds to projected rather than current inflation, its long-run response is larger, and there is no evidence of momentum in its rate changes. On the October 2026 house forecasts (2.0% anchor, German growth as the activity input), the path averages –% over five years and –% over ten, with the deposit rate staying close to 2.50% before easing to 2.25% in 2027–28. The house inflation figure for 2026 needs one decision before the path is used. Read as euro-area HICP, 2.7% requires inflation to fall from 3.8% in September to about 2.4% in the fourth quarter; read as German HICP, it is consistent with the data. The largest judgement is whether the ECB reacts to projected headline inflation, which base effects pull below target in late 2027, or to core. Market pricing of about three more hikes is consistent with the second.

1. Purpose and what differs from the US

The cash leg of the capital markets line needs the expected average return on euro cash over five and ten years, derived from house forecasts rather than copied from market forwards. The case for that, and for the error-correction structure, is set out in the US paper and is not repeated here. Money-market futures embed premia that are hard to separate from expectations in the euro area as in the US (Ferrero and Nobili 2009), and a forecast-driven path leaves room for a house view.

Four things differ. The policy instrument is the deposit facility rate (DFR). It is a single rate rather than a target range, and the ECB has steered the stance through it since its March 2024 operational-framework decision (European Central Bank 2024). The reaction-function evidence comes from a shorter history, mostly 1999–2014, and points to a forward-looking rule with no momentum. The house activity input is German real GDP, not euro-area GDP, because that is how the house models are set up. And the starting point sits inside an energy shock. Headline HICP was 3.8% in September 2026 with energy up 18.9% and core at 2.5%. The ECB raised the DFR by 25bp in June and September to 2.50% and held on 29 October (European Central Bank 2026b).

2. What the literature supports

2.1 The ECB's reaction function

Before the euro existed, Gerlach and Schnabel (2000) found that the GDP-weighted average of three-month rates in the future member states over 1990–98 followed a Taylor rule closely, with an inflation response of 1.58 and an output-gap response of 0.45; they could not reject Taylor's 1.5 and 0.5. Estimates on ECB data since 1999 share one finding: the ECB is described well by a rule on expected inflation and poorly by one on current inflation. Gerdesmeier and Roffia (2004), using real-time data for 1999–2003, estimate an inflation response of 0.39 on current data, which fails the Taylor principle, against 1.31 on SPF forecasts twelve months ahead and 2.91 on forecasts 24 months ahead. Sauer and Sturm (2007) reach the same conclusion: contemporaneous rules suggest an accommodating ECB, while forward-looking rules and rules on survey expectations imply a stabilising one. Gorter, Jacobs and de Haan (2008) find realised inflation insignificant but Consensus Economics expectations significant. In a later update on the same data they estimate a long-run response of 1.39–1.43 to expected inflation and 0.83–1.52 to expected growth (Gorter, Jacobs and de Haan 2009).

The most careful estimate uses the Eurosystem and ECB staff projections available at each meeting. Paloviita et al. (2021), for 1999Q4–2014Q2, find quarterly smoothing of 0.84, a long-run response of 4.45 to inflation projected four quarters ahead and 1.48 to projected growth. They also find a de facto inflation aim of 1.77% (95% interval 1.62–1.91) and a response to past deviations from target. Gerlach and Lewis (2014), on monthly data to 2011 with SPF expectations, find a long-run inflation response of 4.55. Model-based estimates are lower. The posterior mode of the ECB's New Area-Wide Model II gives 2.75–2.93 (Coenen et al. 2018; Darracq Pariès, Kornprobst and Priftis 2026). Smets and Wouters (2003), in their estimated euro-area DSGE model, give 1.68. These estimates span 1.2 to 4.6. The single-equation real-time estimates sit at the top of that range, and their samples end before the 2021–23 inflation surge, for which no estimated DFR rule is yet available.

The ECB's stated strategy bears on the calibration. Since 2021 the target has been 2% over the medium term, and the 2025 assessment confirmed it as symmetric. It "recognises the importance of appropriately forceful or persistent monetary policy action in response to large, sustained deviations of inflation from the target in either direction", with the response depending on "the origin, magnitude and persistence of the deviation" (European Central Bank 2025a). A de facto aim below 2% (Paloviita et al. 2021) belongs to the pre-2021 regime, so we set π* at 2.0%.

2.2 Growth rather than the output gap

The euro-area evidence favours growth over the level of the output gap more clearly than the US evidence does. Gerlach (2007), studying ECB decisions over 1999–2006, finds expected real GDP growth and economic sentiment highly significant and the output gap insignificant. In NAWM II the response to the change in the output gap is 0.09–0.10 a quarter, three times the long-run response to its level, which on impact is close to zero (Coenen et al. 2018). In Smets and Wouters (2003) the change in the gap carries 0.16 a quarter, against a long-run response of 0.10 to the level. Orphanides and Wieland (2013) go further. A first-difference rule, raising the rate by half the gap between the SPF inflation forecast and the aim and by half the gap between forecast growth and the Commission's real-time potential growth, is “similar to the evolution of actual ECB policy”, with no output-gap level and no neutral rate. Hartmann and Smets (2018) estimate that rule on staff projections and find coefficients "somewhat smaller than, but not significantly different from 0.5". Paloviita et al. (2021) also use projected growth, citing the real-time mismeasurement of the output gap and the way the ECB communicates.

We therefore keep the US design, with growth minus potential in place of the gap, and the anchor supplying the level information a pure difference rule lacks. The long-run growth response is about 1.4: Paloviita et al. (2021) estimate 1.25–1.69, and NAWM II's change-in-gap coefficient divided by its adjustment speed gives about 1.4.

2.3 Inertia without momentum

Quarterly smoothing in the real-time single-equation estimates is 0.77–0.84 (Paloviita et al. 2021), and the monthly 0.93–0.94 of Gerlach and Lewis (2014) is 0.80–0.83 a quarter. In our notation that is an adjustment speed of 0.16–0.23. NAWM II's 0.92–0.93 and Smets and Wouters' 0.96 imply slower adjustment, 0.04–0.08, though DSGE smoothing coefficients also absorb the persistence of shocks the model does not otherwise capture. The real-time estimates are the more relevant guide to how quickly the rate moves towards its target in practice, so we take 0.17 with a range of 0.07–0.25. Gerdesmeier and Roffia's (2004) much faster adjustment comes from a four-year sample and is not used.

Unlike the Fed, the ECB shows no momentum in its rate changes. Gerlach and Lewis (2014) find the lagged change negative and insignificant (−0.14, p = 0.13), and in Gerlach's (2007) ordered-probit model of decisions the lagged change enters negatively. We set momentum to zero. The upper end of its range, 0.25, comes from US estimates and has no euro-area support. With no momentum, convergence has no overshoot, and a gap halves in about 4 quarters (Figure 4).

2.4 Which inflation, and at what horizon

Every ECB estimate that finds a stabilising policy uses forecasts, and the horizons cluster at about a year: four quarters in Paloviita et al. (2021), eleven months in Hartmann and Smets (2018), twelve months in Gerlach and Lewis (2014) and Orphanides and Wieland (2013), and three quarters in Bletzinger and Wieland (2017). Growth horizons are shorter, one to three quarters. The rule therefore responds to the house forecast of headline HICP four quarters ahead and of growth two quarters ahead. Both leads can be changed in the lab. This matches the ECB's medium-term orientation, which gives it "flexibility to look through temporary shocks to inflation that may dissipate on their own accord", provided expectations stay anchored. The 2025 assessment adds that "pre-emptive action to avert de-anchoring may be necessary because it is very costly to counteract once it has occurred" (European Central Bank 2025b).

The forecast lead looks through energy only if the house forecast does. The estimated rules respond to projected headline HICP, so when oil is expected to fall and the 2026 spike drops out of the twelve-month comparison, projected headline falls below target and the rule eases. The 2021–23 episode shows the risk. Tatar and Wieland (2025) find that rules on realised core or domestic inflation would have called for tightening in 2021, well before the ECB began. The ECB's own review concludes that "any initial delay in policy normalisation was arguably compensated by a more forceful normalisation subsequently" (Workstream on Monetary Policy Tools, Strategy and Communication 2025). The September 2026 staff projections had headline at 2.5% and core at 2.6% for 2027, and the ECB raised rates at the same meeting. It has said that "the longer energy prices stay high, the more likely they are to drive up broader inflation through indirect and second-round effects" (European Central Bank 2026c). That behaviour sits closer to a response to projected core than to projected headline. Our default follows the estimated rules (headline, θ = 0), and the core-reactive alternative (θ = 1) is a scenario. In the current episode it is the largest judgement in the framework (Section 6).

2.5 Anchoring and mean reversion

The long-run DFR is a house input, here 2.00%, with long-run HICP inflation of 2.0%, so a long-run real rate of zero. That sits in the middle of the ECB staff range for nominal r* of 1¾–2¼%. The staff describe these ranges as "merely indicative", and including Holston–Laubach–Williams-type models widens the range to 1¾–3% (Brand, Lisack and Mazelis 2025). The US paper's argument for setting rather than estimating the anchor applies with more force here, given how far euro-area estimates have moved since the 2018 staff review, when most estimates of the real rate were negative (Brand, Bielecki and Penalver 2018). With all gaps closed, a deviation from the anchor decays as xt = (1 + ρ − λ)xt−1 − ρxt−2. The consistency conditions are the same as for the US. Long-run inflation in the forecasts must equal π*, long-run growth must equal g*, and 1 + φπ must exceed one.

2.6 Implementation: steps, meetings and the lower bound

The DFR moves in multiples of 25bp at eight monetary-policy meetings a year. Only 17 December remains in 2026, and the 2027 calendar has two meetings in each quarter. The rule runs on a continuous intended rate, and the implemented rate moves on the 25bp grid, at most 25bp per meeting. Because the 2.0% anchor is itself a grid point, the US artefact of a path parking either side of a range boundary does not arise. The 25bp cap is a simplification: in 2022 the ECB moved by 50bp and 75bp. Steps apply over the whole horizon; because the anchor is a grid point, the stepped path converges to it exactly. The lower bound is −0.50%, the lowest DFR to date (from September 2019), which is also the value ECB staff use in strategy simulations (Mazelis, Motto and Ristiniemi 2023). As in the US version, the intended rate may fall below it, so the rule lifts off later after a lower-bound episode.

2.7 German growth as the activity input

The ECB responds to euro-area conditions, so German growth relative to German potential stands in for the euro-area growth gap only if the two cycles move together. Measuring the gap against potential removes the structural difference: German potential growth is lower than the euro area's, estimated by the Bundesbank at 0.3–0.4% a year (Deutsche Bundesbank 2026) and by the joint economic forecast at about 0.2%, falling (Projektgruppe Gemeinschaftsdiagnose 2026a). Idiosyncratic German shocks are not removed. In 2025 German GDP grew 0.2% against 1.4% for the euro area, and German fiscal expansion is expected to lift German growth relative to the euro area in 2027–28. The house long-run growth rate, 1.0% a year, serves as potential in the rule and is well above the official German estimates. If true potential is nearer 0.4%, the house path of 1.1–1.2% in 2027–28 implies a larger positive gap, and the rule would hold rates higher than shown. We recommend replacing the input with euro-area GDP once the house models produce it. In the meantime, scenarios should be read as German-cycle scenarios.

4. Inputs and conventions

German growth in 2026. Price- and calendar-adjusted German GDP was 0.7% and 1.0% above a year earlier in the first two quarters of 2026. If the third quarter stagnated, as the Bundesbank expects, it was about 1.0% up on a year earlier. A 1.0% annual average then needs the fourth quarter to be 1.3% above a year earlier, a quarterly rise of about 0.6%. That is a strong rebound but not implausible, and it is far closer to the data than the 0.3% of the previous round. Forecasts published in the autumn put 2026 at 1.3% (Projektgruppe Gemeinschaftsdiagnose 2026b).

The 2026 inflation figure. Euro-area HICP averaged 2.8% over January–September 2026 and was 3.8% in September. A 2.7% average for 2026 then needs the fourth quarter to average about 2.4%. That would take a fall in energy prices of more than 10% within the quarter, while Brent rose about 11% in October to about $115. German HICP, by contrast, averaged 2.3%, 2.7% and 3.0% in the first three quarters, so 2.7% for 2026 implies a fourth quarter of about 2.8%, consistent with the data. The figure therefore looks like a German inflation forecast, matching the German GDP input. Run against euro-area history, the annual-average conversion produces a sharp dip in the fourth quarter followed by a rebound in 2027. With the rule reading inflation four quarters ahead, that rebound triggers a December hike that is an artefact of the conversion. The second scenario reads the figure as German HICP. In that case the rule reads German inflation as a proxy for euro-area inflation, which has recently run 0.3–0.5pp below it. The monthly euro-area HICP builder (third scenario) gives the data-consistent euro-area path: with energy prices up about 3% in October and then falling about 7% a year, roughly the futures curve, euro-area HICP averages about 3.1% in 2026 and 2.5% in 2027 and dips to about 1.5% at end-2027 as the March–April 2026 energy spike drops out. The September ECB staff projections, which assumed Brent at $89.5 in 2026 and $78 in 2027, had 3.0% and 2.5% (European Central Bank 2026a).

Potential and consistency. Long-run German growth of 1.0% is used as potential, and long-run HICP equals the 2% target, so the path settles at the anchor. Both house inputs are internally consistent. Whether 1.0% is the right potential is the question raised in 2.7.

5. Results

–

All scenarios run with the current parameters and anchor. Year-end values are the deposit facility rate where the path is stepped.

Three results stand out across the scenarios. First, the house forecasts imply little further tightening. Read as German inflation, the deposit rate holds at 2.50% and eases to 2.25% by end-2027. Read against euro-area history, the conversion artefact adds a December hike that is reversed in 2027. Second, the data-consistent euro-area HICP path, read four quarters ahead, takes the rule below target through 2027, so it eases to 2.25% by end-2026 and settles near 2.0%. Third, a rule that responds to projected core inflation, or one facing oil that stays near $115, raises the deposit rate to 3.00–3.25% in 2027. That is close to the roughly three further hikes priced in money markets at the end of October. As in the previous round, the gap between the house path and market pricing is mostly a disagreement about oil and about how the ECB treats energy, not about growth.

6. Sensitivity

Table 2 moves each parameter to the ends of its evidence range for the selected scenario. As in the US, the adjustment speed changes timing but moves ten-year averages by a few basis points. The anchor moves them almost one for one. The inflation response matters more here than in the US because its range is wide (1.4–4.5) and because the 2027 base effects create a large projected inflation gap. In the current episode the inflation measure, headline or core, and its horizon are the decisive judgements.

7. Limitations and next steps

The rule is calibrated, not estimated, and the evidence it rests on ends before the 2021–23 surge and the 2024–25 easing. The first check is a back-test on SPF or staff projections over 1999–2026, recording where the rule misses: the 2008 and 2011 hikes, the negative-rate period and the late start in 2022 are the obvious candidates. The ECB's balance-sheet policies and the fragmentation tools that accompanied the 2022 tightening are outside the rule; persistent deviations of that kind belong in scenarios. The activity input should move to euro-area GDP, and the German potential-growth assumption should be reconciled with official estimates. Neither the anchor nor its determinants are modelled here.
